## Everyday Analogy & Why It Matters

Open compilers, web servers, and code libraries create trillions of dollars in business value without charging developers a toll every time a program compiles. Treating every routine code edit as a fresh cloud conversation confuses value with rent. When an AI solves a class of problem once and engineers lock that verified fix into a fast local tool, repeated work drops toward raw electricity cost.

At list prices, a single multi-step coding query can cost over two dollars and burn a thousand times more words than simple chat, even when extra words fail to raise accuracy. Paying that bill forever on routine tasks ignores software's greatest strength: reuse. Our essay labels every published figure strictly by what it counts.

Multiplying thirty million developers by one hundred thousand dollars yields a three-trillion-dollar value projection, whereas surveyed enterprise spend on AI coding tools in 2025 was four billion dollars across actual buyers. First, open weights and competition have cut the price of fixed benchmark capability roughly thirteenfold per year since 2023. Second, crystallizing checked answers into deterministic local routines slashes how many paid model calls each recurring software job even requires.

Software creation can grow massively valuable for society and everyday builders while perpetual per-word vendor revenue compresses steadily toward the physical cost of electricity.

## Inside the Mechanism & Candid Limits

Venture and consulting slides arrive at trillions by multiplying roughly thirty million professional developers—or six times that many open-source accounts—by assumed hundred-thousand-dollar output gains across the world economy. Meanwhile, actual buyer surveys put 2025 enterprise coding-tool spend at four billion dollars, and leading coding assistants report annualized run rates in the single-digit billions. At published list rates, one agentic coding query with a hundred-thousand-word context and ten thousand output words costs two dollars and twenty-five cents—nearly ten thousand dollars a year per developer at three queries an hour. Companies blow through annual budgets by spring because every recurring migration or bug fix is billed from scratch.

To stop blending forecasts with bank statements, our study tags every single claim with one of five strict labels: assumption-driven projections, company-reported run rates and valuations, buyer surveys, audited regulatory filings such as multi-billion-dollar quarterly equity losses, and reproducible measured runs whose artifacts are kept. Even without reuse, fierce competition and open weights have driven the price of reaching a fixed coding benchmark down roughly ten to thirteen times per year since 2023. Verified reuse adds a second, steeper drop: once a checked fix becomes a deterministic local routine, every repeat arrival needs zero model calls. In our three-dimensional global compute model, large cloud models remain genuinely valuable when exploring unfamiliar specifications for the first time, while recurring code transformations crystallize into tiny checked programs on local machines.

Economic value shifts away from metered word volume and toward verified problem closure. Compilers, relational databases, and open operating systems created trillions of dollars in global economic surplus without charging programmers a toll every time code compiled. Measured studies across eight coding models already show agent loops burning a thousand times more words than chat, varying thirtyfold per run without improving accuracy. Our essay makes no stock-market predictions and claims no external hardware power meter.

Instead, it sets out clean, refutable propositions on public numbers: if verified software reuse works as engineering expects, model calls, dollars, and joules per merged repository change will fall steadily toward local electricity cost even as world software output expands.
