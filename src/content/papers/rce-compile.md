# RCE Compile: A Law of Compilation, Tested as a Normal Form Against Real Compilers

*{{revision}}. Research study, draft. Project repository: [openIE-dev/rce, `compile/`](https://github.com/openIE-dev/rce/tree/main/compile), which holds the charter, the hypotheses and the experiments this study reports.*

## Abstract

RCE Compile is a research project that asks whether a single statement of what compilation is, here called a Law of Compilation, holds for any compiler, independent of the Reality Code Engine (RCE) in whose repository the project lives. The candidate statement says that compilation is meaning-preserving lowering between canonical representations, ranked by a cost whose floor is Landauer's limit and whose target is the shortest equivalent program, gated by verification and compounded by reuse. This study checks that candidate against twelve families of production compilers and the research literature behind them. The candidate fails as a description: no compiler surveyed hashes a name-free representation, lowering in shipping compilers is neither total nor shown to preserve meaning, and no compiler accounts for energy. It holds up as a normative framework once three clauses are rewritten, and the study states it in that form: a normal form with two bounds, plus a measured distance from them. The convergence function cannot be computed or measured as stated, and the study replaces it with two counts that can be computed and compared with measured energy. Seven hypotheses with falsifiers follow, with the three experiments run so far and the protocols of two more. Compiler outputs were compared byte for byte on three machines; some differences survive the toolchains' path remapping and have no traced cause, so the hypothesis that lowering is deterministic is neither supported nor falsified. Erasure and description length were counted for 96 builds on a machine with no energy counter, so no energy has been read. A name-free hash, the part of the standard that no compiler surveyed has, was built in two forms and matched renamed copies of programs more often than keys over output bytes or source text, and matched no builds of two different kernels, on 16 small kernels and 4 programs; that supports the registered hypothesis for this corpus only. Almost every external source was seen only as a search result, and each citation says so.

**In plain words**

1. The question is whether all compilers obey one rule. Real compilers do not, so the rule is restated as a standard that any compiler can be measured against.
2. The standard has seven parts. Most compilers have some of them. None keeps track of energy, and none proves that it translates every program correctly.
3. The physical and mathematical limits in the original rule are real but cannot be computed, so they are replaced by two quantities that can be counted and compared with an energy meter.
4. One missing part has now been built and tested: a fingerprint of a compiled program that ignores the names in it. On a small set of test programs it recognised renamed copies more often than fingerprints of the raw bytes or of the source text did, and it never gave the same fingerprint to two test kernels that compute different things.

## 1. Problem statement

A compiler takes a program in one language and produces a program in another, usually closer to a machine. There are thousands of compilers, built by communities that rarely share vocabulary: systems languages, scientific computing, legacy business languages, high-integrity languages, functional languages and the compilers for neural-network tensor programs. Each community has its own account of what a correct and good compiler is. RCE Compile asks whether one statement covers all of them, independent of RCE, and if not, what the nearest statement is that does.

The candidate under test came out of an earlier discussion with an assistant system (Grok). It says: **compilation is semantics-preserving lowering between canonical representations, ranked by a cost whose floor is Landauer's limit and whose target is Kolmogorov-minimal, gated by verification, and compounded by reuse.** Three questions follow. Does it describe the compilers that exist? If not, can it be restated so that it is true of a correct compiler and checkable against a real one? And can its physical and mathematical terms be measured at all? The discussion that proposed the candidate also made 55 factual claims about the field; those are checked in [Appendix A](#appendix-a-claims-checked).

## 2. The candidate and its refinement

### 2.1 Why the candidate fails as a description

The candidate makes three kinds of claim: about representation ("canonical"), about lowering ("semantics-preserving"), and about cost ("floor is Landauer, target is Kolmogorov-minimal"). Each fails as a description of shipping compilers, for reasons given in full in §3 and §4:

- **No compiler surveyed hashes a name-free intermediate representation.** Every one keys its identity and its caches on named or textual content.
- **Lowering is neither total nor proved to preserve meaning.** Fuzzers have found more than 325 bugs (Csmith) and 1,602 bugs (the EMI project) in production C compilers [1] [2], and Zig's default self-hosted backend fails 24 of its 2,008 behaviour tests [3].
- **No compiler accounts for energy.** Every cost model found is in cycles, bytes or node counts.
- **The convergence function cannot be computed or measured as stated.** Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable; even the minimum heat of one fixed circuit is PP-hard to compute [4]; and today's hardware sits $10^6$ to $10^{11}$ times above the Landauer floor.

A statement that every compiler is claimed to obey, and that real compilers measurably break, is falsified as a descriptive law. No peer-reviewed source in English, Chinese, Russian or Japanese was found that names or proves a single law of compilation [NOT FOUND]; an English search for the phrase returns only the copyright sense of "compilation" [5].

### 2.2 The refinement: a normal form with two bounds and a measured distance

The candidate holds up much better as a normative framework: a statement of what a correct, well-ranked compiler must satisfy, against which every real compiler can be placed and its shortfall measured. Three clauses change. "Semantics-preserving" becomes a **declared contract**, because the established notion is refinement under a precondition and the precondition differs by compiler. "Canonical" becomes an **operation** the framework applies to any representation, because no compiler supplies it. And "Landauer floor and Kolmogorov target" becomes **two computable counts plus a measurement**. The result is a normal form in the sense of Hoare, He and Sampaio (§6), with two bounds and a measured distance from them, not a law every compiler already obeys.

### 2.3 The normative statement

The project's charter states the standard as follows; the text is quoted from it unchanged [6]:

> **Compiler Form.** A compiler is a partial function from declared inputs (source, flags, toolchain, and any build path after remapping) to a chain of representations R₀ → … → Rₙ. Each step either stops or produces an output that a named checker K accepts as preserving a declared contract: a property class, an equivalence level, a linking assumption and a trusted computing base. Among accepted outputs, a stated cost C ranks the candidates. Reuse U is keyed by a name-free hash of the representation together with its contract. Nothing is released unless G records K's acceptance. For every released output, the framework reports its distance from two bounds, the bits irreversibly overwritten per run (scaled by k<sub>B</sub>·T·ln 2) and a stated description-length proxy, next to the energy measured in the regime the hardware belongs to.

A *stop* is a lowering step that ends with a diagnostic and no output. A *declared input* is an input the compiler's contract lists. The statement drops "holds for any compiler" in favour of "every compiler can be placed against it". The project claims a law only for what its registered hypotheses (§7) survive. §3 sets out the parts the statement names, and §4 the two bounds.

## 3. The Compiler Form, R to G

### 3.1 The seven parts

The project calls the framework the **Compiler Form**. It has seven parts:

| Part | Name | What the part requires |
|---|---|---|
| R | Representation | A canonical IR with a stated equivalence relation and a name-free hash |
| L | Lowering | Each step either stops (ends with a diagnostic and no output) or produces an output that preserves a declared contract |
| C | Cost | A stated cost that ranks equivalent candidates |
| K | Checker | A named checker that certifies the contract |
| U | Reuse | Earlier accepted results are reused, keyed by the name-free hash and the contract |
| E | Energy | Energy is accounted for, by counting or by meter, for each released output |
| G | Gate | Nothing is released unless the gate records the checker's acceptance |

Two refinements, from the correctness and ML-compiler literature, make L, K and G precise enough to test.

### 3.2 Four equivalence levels

ML compilers define correctness as closeness. XLA, TorchInductor, TVM, TensorRT, Triton and IREE all test against a reference within an absolute and relative tolerance (PyTorch's float32 defaults are a relative tolerance of $1.3\times10^{-6}$ and an absolute tolerance of $10^{-5}$) [K], because fusion, tiling, split reductions and tensor-core accumulation reorder floating-point sums, and lowering to FP8 or FP4 changes outputs on purpose [K]. FlexAttention's FlashAttention-4 backward pass is reported to be nondeterministic when block sparsity is on [7]. If "preserving" meant identical output bits, these compilers would all violate L. A September 2026 preprint argues that bit-level results depend on the accumulation structure the compiler chooses, reconstructs that structure for cuBLAS across three GPU generations, and gives static checkers that decide bitwise equivalence across tens of thousands of autotuner configurations [8]; MMA-Sim gives a bit-accurate model of tensor and matrix cores on ten architectures [9]. Halide already shows the pattern at small scale: a schedule cannot change results except through named reassociating reductions [K].

The Form therefore requires every lowering to declare one of four **equivalence levels**, and its checker to certify that level:

| Level | Meaning | What K must certify |
|---|---|---|
| Exact | Integer, Boolean or fixed-point; identical output bits | Bitwise equality |
| Bitwise within an accumulation class | Floating point, with the reduction order recorded in R; L and C choose only within the class | Same class, then bitwise equality |
| Within a declared error bound | Floating point, with an absolute, relative or ULP bound | The bound, by static error analysis or interval methods, not by sample tests |
| Declared lossy | Quantisation or precision lowering | Nothing about equality: it is a change of program, recorded and admitted by G as a separate decision |

The default is "exact unless a checked contract says otherwise". That is stricter than every production ML compiler and consistent with the bitwise-class research.

### 3.3 What a gate must name

The classical correctness theorem is not "the output means the same as the input". CompCert's main theorem is **behaviour refinement under a precondition**: if compilation succeeds and the source has no undefined behaviour, every observable behaviour of the compiled code is a behaviour of the source [K]. Patterson and Ahmed show that "the compiler is correct" is ambiguous until the theorem says what target code the output may be linked with [10]. Secure compilation adds that a compiler can preserve meaning in CompCert's sense and still let an attacker at the target level break abstractions of the source: *full abstraction* (source-equivalent components compile to target-equivalent ones) and the *robust property preservation* criteria are distinct, stronger guarantees [11]. And CompCert's proof leaves the preprocessor, assembler and linker outside it; Csmith found silent miscompilations exactly there and none in the verified middle end [1].

From these, every gate in the Form must state three things:

1. **The property class it preserves**: trace properties, hyperproperties, a robust variant, or an equivalence.
2. **The linking assumption**: which other code the output may be combined with.
3. **The trusted base**: the semantics, proof kernel, assembler, hardware models or diverse compiler the guarantee relies on.

Fully abstract compilation usually needs run-time checks or isolation, so a stronger property class raises cost [K].

On 2 November 2026, before any gate was measured, the project's charter added two statements to these three [6]:

4. **The authority of its evidence**: the level that the evidence behind the checker's acceptance must have.
5. **The digests its verdict is bound to**: the SHA-256 (the *digest*) of the declared inputs, of the output it releases and of the checker that accepted it, and the revision of the contract it checked. A verdict bound to a digest applies only to the exact bytes it was made on.

The charter takes the levels of authority from the reconstruction ledgers of REA (Reverse Engineer Anything), an open-source reverse-engineering project [6]. Two levels are *observed* evidence: *pinned*, in which the compiler, named by the SHA-256 of its toolchain, is run on the declared inputs and its output compared byte for byte; and *replay*, in which an output recorded earlier and bound by its SHA-256 is compared byte for byte. *Proved* evidence is a checker's certificate for every input of its fragment: a proof that a proof kernel checks, a translation validator's verdict, or an SMT solver's finding that the negated contract cannot be satisfied. *Derived* evidence follows from a stated rule that cites its source, such as a documented gate behaviour quoted with its passage. *Inferred* evidence is the statement of a model, a heuristic or a stochastic proposer, or a summary that cites no record of a higher level. A gate accepts an output only when evidence of the authority it states holds and is bound to every digest it states; a verdict bound to other bytes, or evidence that cannot be read, releases nothing. Inferred evidence never makes a gate accept. Observed evidence shows that a contract held, or failed, on the inputs that were run; only proved evidence certifies it for every input of a fragment. A gate that does not state all five is recorded as a gate with an unstated guarantee.

### 3.5 Part-by-part findings

**R is absent everywhere as the Form states it.** Every production compiler lowers through a chain of IRs rather than one canonical form. Zig goes AST → ZIR → AIR → per-backend MIR [3]; rustc goes AST → HIR → THIR → MIR → one of LLVM IR, Cranelift's CLIF or GCC's GIMPLE [12]; Go goes syntax → IR → SSA → Plan 9-style instructions [13]; GHC goes Core → STG → Cmm [K]. Canonical *forms* exist (GHC Core, LFortran's ASR, Futhark's SOACS, Lean's LCNF, Flang's FIR and HLFIR), and the e-classes of an e-graph are canonical *sets* of equivalent terms. The closest result to the "canonical representations" clause is Bryant's thesis, which treats compilation as normalisation to a normal form [14]. Two qualifications follow. Morris's correctness square needs only *some* semantics on each side, not canonicity [K]. And round-trip holds only between an IR and its own serialised form (LLVM's text and bitcode, CLIF text, MLIR's generic form), never back to source, because every compiler discards comments, names and syntactic sugar [K]. The falsifier is plain: GHC's interface fingerprints, OCaml's interface digests, rustc's stable hashes, Go's action IDs, Julia's source hashes, Lean's Lake traces and Taichi's kernel hashes are all hashes of named or textual content [K]. The Wasm binary comes closest, because names sit in an optional custom section [K]. So R has to become an operation the framework applies to any IR (rename bound names to positions, strip paths, then hash), not a property compilers are claimed to have. The project's experiment E5 implements that operation in two forms and measures them on a small corpus (§8.3).

**L fails as a description; the bug counts are the evidence.** Csmith found more than 325 previously unknown bugs in C compilers [1]. EMI found 147 confirmed bugs in its first 11 months and 1,602 GCC and LLVM bugs in total, 1,007 of them fixed [2]. Alive2, a bounded translation validator for LLVM, found 47 new bugs (28 fixed) and prompted 8 patches to the LLVM language reference; a follow-up encoding of LLVM's memory model found 21 more [15]. Zig's self-hosted x86-64 backend, the default for Debug builds since 0.15, passes 1,984 of 2,008 behaviour tests (the project's development log gives 1,987 against 1,980 for LLVM; the two sources disagree) [3], so lowering is not total in a shipping compiler. CompCert and CakeML show that L can hold as a theorem: CakeML carries its guarantee down to machine code for five instruction sets and has compiled itself inside the HOL4 logic [16]. Neither is a production compiler for the languages surveyed. L survives only as a norm: a step either produces output its checker accepts or stops, and production compilers are measured by how far they fall short.

**C is present but local.** Every compiler has cost models, but almost all are local heuristics: inline budgets, LLVM's target cost queries and scheduling models, GCC's cost hooks [K]. Cranelift's acyclic e-graph is the only production case found that ranks the members of an explicit equivalence class and extracts the cheapest, and it deliberately saturates once, within a budget [17]. Research equality saturation shows uneven gains: TENSAT reports up to 16% [18], and equality saturation inside XLA averages 3.45% with a 56.26% peak on one model [19]. Only one mechanised result about a pass's *performance* guarantee was found, for an inlining heuristic in Rocq [20]. Every cost found is in cycles, latency, bytes or node counts; none is in joules, and the shortest program is not the cheapest whenever unrolling, vectorising or table lookup makes code longer but faster [K].

**K is the strongest part, but it guards the source more than the lowering.** Type systems, Rust's borrow checker and new trait solver [21], GHC's Core Lint, LFortran's ASR verifier, MLIR op verifiers, Futhark's typed IR, Lean's kernel, SPARK's GNATprove proof levels [22] and Erlang's code validator [K] are all real checkers. Three qualifications matter. Checking of the lowering itself exists only in pieces: Alive2 for some LLVM peepholes, SMT verification of some Cranelift ISLE rules, the mechanised Wasm specification [K]. Many IR checkers are debug-only: Core Lint is opt-in and the LLVM verifier between passes is commonly off in release builds [K]. And bounded checkers can miss bugs: Alive2 unrolls loops to a bound [15], and EMI and Csmith find bugs but cannot certify their absence. The ML compilers' checks are tolerance tests on sample inputs, which are tests, not certificates.

**U is the part most often present, but its keys hash names and paths.** Go's build cache maps an action ID (a hash of every input to a step) to an output [23]; rustc uses incremental fingerprints; LLVM's ThinLTO cache, Wasmtime's module cache, JAX's compilation cache, Triton's kernel cache and TVM's tuning database are all keyed by hashes [K]. Reuse of verified work compounds trust: CakeML compiled its own verified binary inside the logic [16]; "Compiling by Proving" turns proofs into optimised rewrite rules [24]; and DDC counters the trusting-trust attack under stated assumptions [25]. Two qualifications remain. The keys hash names and paths, so equal programs with different names miss the cache. And TensorRT-LLM abandoned its serialised, verified-once engines for PyTorch as its only backend [26]: stored artefacts lost to a flexible eager path when hardware and models changed faster than the artefacts could be rebuilt.

**E is absent everywhere.** No compiler in any of the twelve families accounts for energy [K; NOT FOUND for any counterexample]. Energy is measured outside compilers: by machine (the June 2026 Green500 leader runs at 73.28 GFlops per watt [27]) and by counters such as RAPL and NVML [K]. Research on energy-aware compilation is modest: the choice of compiler flags shifts energy by tens of percent, and energy and run time are "roughly correlated, though not always" [28]; a 2025 Fortran study found "minimal difference" in energy between gfortran, ifx and ifort, tracking run time [29]. The only compiler found that treats energy as a first-class quantity is the research language Eel, which treats reversible operations as free and charges for bit creation and destruction [30]. Either E is not a necessary part of a law of compilation, or the whole field falls short of it. This study takes the second reading: E is a part the framework adds, supplied by an external meter.

**G is present only in some compilers.** rustc emits no code after a type or borrow error; Go refuses unused imports and variables; Wasm engines refuse an invalid module at load time; Erlang's validator checks generated code before writing it [K]. SPARK is a gate only when a project's CI requires GNATprove with no unproved checks; the compiler itself does not refuse [K]. Elsewhere G is off or partial. tsc emits JavaScript despite type errors unless `noEmitOnError` is set; LLVM and GCC ship from trunk on post-commit testing and reverts rather than a gate before merging; Zig analyses lazily, so code that is never referenced is never checked [K]. ML compilers have fallbacks rather than gates: a graph break in TorchDynamo falls back to eager Python unless `fullgraph=True` turns it into an error, and ONNX Runtime sends unsupported nodes to its CPU provider [K].

## 4. The convergence function

### 4.1 Landauer and Kolmogorov as conceptual bounds

The candidate's convergence function says that the cost of a compiled program has a floor at Landauer's limit and a target at Kolmogorov-minimal size. **Landauer's limit** is the minimum heat, $k_B T \ln 2$, released into surroundings at temperature $T$ when one bit is erased: about 2.871 zeptojoules at 300 K (2.853 at 298.15 K) [K, arithmetic]. It bounds heat per bit *erased*, not per operation, and it is reached only in the infinitely slow limit; erasing faster costs more, roughly as $1/\tau$ for erasure time $\tau$ [K]. Single-bit experiments confirm the bound at that limit, in colloidal beads (Bérut et al., 2012) and in nanomagnetic memory (Hong et al., 2016) [31]. **Kolmogorov complexity** $K(x)$ is the length of the shortest program that prints $x$. Both are real physics or mathematics, and as bounds they say something true: no compiled program can dissipate less than its erasures require, and none can be shorter than the shortest equivalent program.

### 4.2 Why neither is computable or measurable as stated

**The Kolmogorov target cannot be computed.** $K(x)$ is uncomputable (Kolmogorov, Chaitin, Solomonoff); compressors give only upper bounds, and program equivalence, the constraint the target sits under, is undecidable in general by Rice's theorem [K]. The full-employment theorem for compiler writers makes this concrete: no compiler can always produce the smallest equivalent program, because that would decide halting [K]. Superoptimisers approach the target for short loop-free fragments under an SMT or test-vector check, but never with a guarantee.

**The Landauer floor cannot be computed either, and it is far below today's costs.** Computing the optimal heat functions for one fixed circuit is PP-complete according to the abstract of a 2026 paper, multiplicative approximation is NP-hard, and only additive approximation has a polynomial-time randomised algorithm [4]; the body reportedly argues PP-hardness, so membership in PP remains to be checked. Choosing among equivalent circuits, which is the compilation problem, sits on top of that. At the commonly quoted 45 nm figures, a 32-bit integer add costs about 0.1 picojoules, **about $3.5\times10^{7}$ times** $k_B T \ln 2$, or about $10^6$ per erased bit if 32 bits are overwritten; a 640-picojoule DRAM read is **about $2.2\times10^{11}$** times the floor; and the overhead of one out-of-order instruction, about 70 picojoules, is **about $2.5\times10^{10}$** [32]. Today's gap is therefore $10^6$ to $10^{11}$, and almost all of it is wires, memory and control, not logical erasure.

**The two terms overlap.** Bennett showed that any computation can be made logically reversible, so the Landauer floor applies only to information a program deliberately discards [K]. Zurek, and Bennett and colleagues, showed that the minimum thermodynamic cost of erasing a string $x$ is about $k_B T \ln 2 \cdot K(x)$, and that converting $x$ to $y$ costs about their information distance [K]. The Landauer cost of erasure is already a Kolmogorov quantity, so minimising both jointly counts the same thing twice. Three further problems remain. Shortness and low energy trade off, so a joint objective without a stated weighting is a Pareto front, not a function. Time is missing from the statement. And the energy spent compiling can outweigh the savings: in an October 2026 study, 42 of 44 configurations of model self-refinement used 4% to 886% more execution energy than the reference solutions, and the two exceptions broke even only after about $10^6$ to $10^7$ executions [33].

One result supports the idea at its core. Wolpert and Kolchinsky's "circuit Landauer loss" shows that two circuits computing the same function can have different minimum dissipation [34], and mismatch-cost results give strictly positive lower bounds for circuits [35]. Circuit structure, which is what a compiler chooses, does change the physical floor.

### 4.3 The measurable replacement

The replacement keeps both terms and makes each computable:

1. **ε, the erasure count**: the number of bits a run overwrites irreversibly, computed from the IR (bits overwritten or discarded without having been copied out). $\varepsilon \cdot k_B T \ln 2$ is the run's Landauer bound.
2. **λ, a declared description-length proxy**: IR node count, binary bytes, compressed bytes, or an MDL code length relative to a named model class. The proxy is declared before a measurement and not changed after it.

Both are computed under an equivalence checked on a decidable fragment, and reported next to **measured joules** in the energy regime the hardware belongs to:

| Regime | Energy is proportional to | Example hardware |
|---|---|---|
| A | Counted operations | In-order cores, FPGAs |
| B | Time multiplied by power | Out-of-order CPUs, GPUs |
| C | Entropy production | Thermodynamic hardware, or a simulation of it; the only regime in which Landauer units apply directly |

Regimes A and B are approximations: static power scales with time even on in-order cores [K]. The claim becomes testable: does lowering ε and λ predict lower measured energy in each regime? No published measurement of the erasure content of real compiled programs against their energy was found [NOT FOUND], so the result would be new evidence whichever way it comes out. Self-published preprints that claim convergence to the Landauer limit at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ are unreviewed, and their rate is a statistic about estimating work, not a rate at which programs approach a floor [36]; they are not cited as support.

## 5. The gear ladder and the 98/2 split

RCE Compile orders its own compilation steps as a **gear ladder** of six rungs, G0 to G5, tried in order of cost. G0 reuses a stored result by its hash; G1 applies rewrite rules proved correct in advance; G2 extracts the cheapest form from an e-graph. These three are deterministic. G3 to G5 are stochastic proposers, and every output they produce must pass the gate before it is released; the charter defines G3 to G5 only as a group, and their separate definitions are open [6]. (The rungs are written G0 to G5; the Form's gate is the part named G.) The design principle attached to the ladder is a split: **at least 98% of the work deterministic, at most 2% stochastic, and everything gated.**

No source measures such a split for any compiler, by decision count, CPU time, cost or energy [NOT FOUND]. The verified systems sit at the two ends instead. Equality saturation, Souper, Cranelift and MLGO are fully deterministic at compile time; MLGO's policy is fixed and compiled into LLVM, and it decides one class of choices (inline or not, which register to evict) for gains of 0.3% to 7% [37]. At the other end, a stochastic author writes nearly everything behind a deterministic gate: in work reported in October 2026, a model wrote about 180 million lines of Rocq proof that the Rocq checker accepts or rejects in full [38], and AdaCore's GNAT Foundry: Intersection demonstrator has an agent write SPARK while deterministic tools produce all the release evidence [39]. By authoring effort that split is inverted, while the gate is total, so any claim about 98/2 must first say what is being counted. One taxonomy places hybrid workflows at 14% to 56% deterministic, but it is a classification, not a measurement [40].

The literature does support the two principles beneath the split. **Gate everything**: SuperCoder's best configuration still fails 5% of its test-checked outputs, and the strongest commercial baseline in that paper passes only 51.5% [41]; STOKE paired a stochastic search with an SMT validator [K]. **Put deterministic reuse first**: Compiled AI moves all stochastic work into a one-time compile phase and breaks even after about 17 runs [42], and Progressive Crystallization promotes recurring agent runs into deterministic workflows [43]. One caution carries over: in Veritas and CktFormalizer the gate checks the stochastic output against its own specification (CNF clauses, hardware types), while an error in the specification sits outside the gate [44] [45].

The 98/2 split is therefore a registered hypothesis, listed in §7, not a measured result. It is to be measured rung by rung with a declared unit and recorded by the gate. Candidate units are accepted decisions, accepted rewrites, CPU seconds and joules; each is reported separately, because they can disagree.

## 6. Prior art

No earlier source found names a single law of compilation (§2.1). The closest earlier claims each cover a few of the seven parts.

- **Morris (1973)**, following Burstall and Landin (1969) and refined by the ADJ group (Thatcher, Wagner and Wright, 1979 to 1981), made compiler correctness a commuting square of algebra homomorphisms: compiling then interpreting equals interpreting then encoding [46] [47]. That is L and K. Lowering as stated in the Form restates it and claims no novelty there.
- **Futamura (1971)** showed that specialising an interpreter on a program yields a compiled program, that specialising the specialiser on the interpreter yields a compiler, and that specialising it on itself yields a compiler generator [48]. **Ershov's** Novosibirsk school reached the same view as "mixed computation" [49]. That is L and U, and the strongest prior claim that compilers are derived rather than designed.
- **Hoare, He and Sampaio (Acta Informatica, 1993)** treated compilation as reducing a source program, by algebraic laws of programming, to a normal form that mirrors the target machine, with correctness following from the laws; the work fed into Hoare and He's *Unifying Theories of Programming* (1998) [50]. This is the closest prior work to calling compilation "governed by laws", and the closest to the normal-form framing this study adopts.
- **Cousot and Cousot (POPL 1977)** unified static analyses as fixpoints over lattices, and later extended this to program transformations [51]. That is K, and the precision ordering in C.
- **Eel (Tyagi, Lynch and Demaine, MIT, 2016)** is the only compiler found that ties code to an energy floor, by charging for bit creation and destruction [30]. That is E.

UNCOL (1958) tried and failed to give one universal R; MLIR answers that failure with many levels but holds no semantics [52]. E-graphs already combine canonical sets, cost-ranked extraction and sound rewrites in one structure [53].

**Two primary sources have not yet been read in full: Hoare, He and Sampaio (1993) [50] and Ershov's "On the essence of compilation" (1977) [54].** Both are cited from memory [K]. Either may contain a precedent for the framing or the wording, and both must be read before RCE Compile claims priority for anything. The novel part that remains, on present evidence, is the combination: one statement binding a physical floor and an information-theoretic target to verified, reuse-compounding lowering, with energy as a ranked quantity.

## 8. Experiments

### 8.1 E1: determinism of lowering, measured

**Method.** E1 tests H1 [6]. Each *configuration* is one compiler with one flag set, and names one or two small programs. For every program, the harness makes four pairs of builds, each build in a fresh directory that holds only the copied source: the same directory twice; two directories with absolute paths of different lengths; the same two directories with the toolchain's documented path remapping; and the same directory twice with `SOURCE_DATE_EPOCH` unset, at least 1.1 s apart. All builds but the last pair run with a declared environment (`LC_ALL=C`, `TZ=UTC`, `SOURCE_DATE_EPOCH=1735689600`), and every build gets new, empty compiler caches, so the second build of a pair is a real rebuild. For each pair the harness records, per output file, the number of byte positions at which the two files differ, and, for an ELF file, how many of those bytes fall in each section. The count is positional, so a path of another length that shifts the rest of a file counts every shifted byte. The remappings are `-ffile-prefix-map` for gcc, g++, clang, clang++, gfortran and gnatmake, `--remap-path-prefix` for rustc, `-trimpath` for Go, `+deterministic` for erlc and `BUILD_PATH_PREFIX_MAP` for ocamlopt; Go 1.21 was described by its developers as "the first Go toolchain with perfectly reproducible builds" [55]. Julia has no remapping in the matrix: its remapped run writes the code without source lines. No remapping was found for Lean, `wasmtime compile`, Zig, GHC or GnuCOBOL, so their remapped comparison is not tested. `go build` is measured three times: in module mode, Go's default; in GOPATH mode with the source file named on the command line; and in GOPATH mode with the source placed in a `GOPATH` and built by its import path, `e1`. For each Go configuration the harness runs the remapped build once more in each directory with `GODEBUG=gocachehash=1`, which makes `go build` print every input it hashes into each action ID, and records the lines that name the build directory. The harness also records the SHA-256 of every output, so that runs on two machines can be compared where a tool's version string is the same on both. Results are written by the harness, and the write-up is generated from them by a script; no figure in it is typed.

**What this means for H1.** The Go GOPATH-mode result is a byte difference that survives remapping across directories, and the project's repository records a verdict on it in the registration's table of changes, dated 2 November 2026 [6]. Its cause is named. Every differing byte lies in the `.note.go.buildid` and `.note.gnu.build-id` sections, and code and data are identical. The `gocachehash` trace shows the compile and link action IDs hashing the build directory, because GOPATH mode gives a package named by a file outside every `GOPATH` the import path `_` followed by its absolute directory, and `-trimpath` does not remove it; once the directory is written as a placeholder, the hashed inputs of the two directories are the same. With only that input changed, the package placed in a `GOPATH` and built by its import path, no hashed input names the directory and the remapped builds are byte-identical across directories, while the original configuration still differs. The verdict applies the registered conditions: the falsifier is a difference that survives remapping when the declared inputs are equal, and here they were not equal, since the import path is an input that `go build` derives from the directory. The result is therefore not a falsifier of H1. The import path can be named and controlled, so it is added to H1's declared inputs, and H1 holds for Go in GOPATH mode with the import path named; for a GOPATH-mode build of a file outside every `GOPATH`, `-trimpath` alone is not a remapping.

The other differences that survive remapping have no recorded verdict. On the CI runner, `gnatmake -O2 -g` and the Julia run differ across directories after remapping, and E1 does not establish whether a declared input differs between the two builds. Across machines, the `clang -O2 -g` and `clang++ -O2 -g` differences after remapping are differences between builds with the same tool version string; E1 records no trace of their cause, and the repository has not recorded a verdict on them. For the macOS laptop, the table of changes records that the run has no verdict yet, and what it establishes [6]. Its remapped builds named the build directory by a path the tools did not record: the system temporary directory, under `/var/folders`, is reached through the symbolic link `/var` to `/private/var`, and Go's trace names the directory with the `/private` prefix. A check on Linux of gcc, clang and rustc found that a remapping that names a linked path leaves the real path in the output. The macOS remapped comparisons therefore do not test H1's remapping condition. Since that run, E1 names the build directory by its real path, attributes differing Mach-O bytes to regions, and adds three configurations that run only on macOS: two in which clang compiles and links in two calls and the linker is told to drop the build directory from its debug map, one of them also setting `ZERO_AR_DATE=1`, which Apple's linker reads to write zero for object-file times [K]; and rustc with the same linker option. No rerun with these changes is in the repository yet; the table of changes names that rerun as the evidence for a verdict. Until those causes are traced, H1 is neither supported nor falsified by E1 as a whole. An earlier probe in the research sessions, with one program per language, had found the same-directory and cross-directory pattern for gcc, clang, rustc, Go in its default mode and gfortran on one machine [M].

**Limits.** The programs are small, one or two per language, so a difference that only a large program reaches is not tested. Link-time optimisation, profile-guided builds and parallel code generation beyond rustc's defaults are not in the matrix. E1 compares bytes; it does not check that meaning was preserved, which is H3 and H7. The macOS run says how many bytes differ in each file, not in which part of it.

### 8.2 E2 to E4: protocols, and E4's counts

E2 and E3 have protocols in the repository and have not been run. E4 has a protocol, a harness and one results file, written on a machine with no energy counter, which holds no joules [6].

**E2, gate audit (H2).** For each compiler, a fixed set of small programs, each with one injected fault and a correct twin that differs only at the fault: a type error for every compiler; a borrow error for rustc; an unused import or variable for Go; an invalid IR module for LLVM's `llc` and an invalid WebAssembly module for `wasmtime compile`; a failing contract (a Lean theorem whose proof does not check, an Ada precondition GNATprove cannot prove); and a generated-code fault for Erlang's validator. Each faulty program and its twin are built under the default flags and under each flag set documented as changing the gate (for example `noEmitOnError` for tsc, `fullgraph=True` for TorchDynamo, `-Werror` for gcc and clang). The harness records the exit status, every artefact written with its SHA-256, and the first line of the diagnostic; the twin must build or the case is void. Each compiler and fault is classified as *emits nothing by default*, *emits by default and nothing when configured*, or *emits always*, and each gate's property class, linking assumption and trusted base is recorded from the compiler's own documentation, quoted, or as "not stated". The falsifier count is the number of cases where observed and documented behaviour differ. The tensor compilers' fallbacks to eager execution or to a CPU provider are recorded as a class of their own. Since the change of 2 November 2026, each record is evidence of authority pinned (§3.3): it is bound to the SHA-256 of the program, of the compiler's executable and of every artefact, and to the flag set, and a record whose digests do not match its case is void and the case is run again. Each gate's documented statements also include the authority of the evidence its checker accepts on and whether that acceptance is recorded bound to digests. A documented statement is derived evidence and is never promoted to an observation, so a compiler's class rests on the observed records; a gate that accepts on inferred evidence, such as a fallback chosen by a heuristic, is recorded with its acceptance as undecided.

**E3, equivalence-level audit (H3).** Each pass or flag is assigned a level before the run: `-O0`, `-O2` and `-O3` exact; `-ffp-contract` settings within a declared error bound; reassociation (`-fassociative-math` with vectorisation, `-ffast-math`, `-Ofast`) within a declared error bound, with the bound stated; and, on GPU runners, split reductions and lowering to FP8 declared lossy. The corpus has integer kernels, exact by construction, and floating-point kernels with sums, dot products, polynomial evaluation and reductions, on fixed inputs that include subnormal numbers, signed zeros, infinities and NaNs (not-a-number values). The exact reference is the same computation in rational arithmetic, rounded once at the end; the declared program is the `-O0` build with strict IEEE 754 semantics. The metrics are the bits that differ, the largest ULP distance, the largest absolute and relative error, and whether a static check (an SMT solver for loop-free code, or an interval or error-analysis tool) certified the level. The static tool is still to be chosen. Since the change of 2 November 2026, each differential-testing record is pinned evidence, bound to the SHA-256 of the kernel's source, the compiler's executable, the built binary and the input: an observed change beyond the assigned level is a counterexample, which is H3's falsifier, and agreement on every input certifies nothing. A level counts as certified only on proved evidence, the static tool's verdict bound to the SHA-256 of the binary or IR it checked, of the tool and of the stated level; a level that only a heuristic or a model assigns is recorded as undecided. The tensor compilers and the FP8 and FP4 formats need GPU runners, which the project does not have yet.

**E4, erasure and description length against measured energy (H4).** The corpus is a set of programs, each compiled under several configurations that E3's checker accepts as equal at the exact level, so that energy differences come from lowering and not from a change of program. ε is counted by instrumenting each run: every write of a register or memory location adds the bits of the previous value that were not copied out before being overwritten. λ is computed with a proxy declared before the run. Joules are measured per run, with intervals, in regime A (an FPGA or in-order core with a supply meter; executed operations as the baseline), regime B (an x86 host with RAPL, or a GPU with NVML; time multiplied by power as the baseline) and regime C (a simulation of entropy production). Compile-time energy is logged too. The analysis is declared in advance: an ordinary least-squares model per regime, with the share of variance explained and a 95% bootstrap interval over programs; ε and λ explain variance when that interval excludes zero. Energy figures without a meter reading are labelled modelled.

**E4, declared before any energy is read** (2 November 2026) [6]. The corpus is eight kernels (`sumsq`, `collatz`, `sieve`, `sort`, `crc32`, `matmul`, `fib` and `popcount`), each a function `run(n)` on 32-bit unsigned integers that uses no library, written in C and in Rust: 16 programs, each built at six optimisation levels (`clang -O0`, `-O1`, `-O2`, `-O3`, `-Os` and `-Oz`; `rustc` at `opt-level` 0, 1, 2, 3, s and z), 96 rows in all. Because E3's checker does not exist yet, the configurations are shown equal by a weaker test: each gives the reference value of its kernel on five declared inputs, natively and as WebAssembly. ε is counted by an interpreter of WebAssembly written for E4 and pinned by its SHA-256, on each kernel built for WebAssembly at the row's level; it is therefore a count for the configuration, not for the native code whose energy is read, and an upper bound, at byte granularity, on the bits a run discards. The primary λ is the number of WebAssembly instructions in the kernel's module. Energy is read in regime B only, from RAPL on Linux and from `powermetrics` on macOS; both are counters reported by the chip or the operating system, not a meter on the supply, and no host of the harness has a counter for regime A or C. The analysis uses adjusted R², which can fall below zero, with a 95% interval from 2,000 resamples of whole programs; ε and λ explain variance when that interval lies above zero, and in regime B H4 is supported only if the interval of the baseline's adjusted R² minus theirs lies above zero, and falsified if it lies below.

**E4, counts without energy.** The harness ran on 2 November 2026 (UTC) on a Linux x86-64 machine (Intel Xeon at 2.80 GHz, 4 CPUs, Ubuntu 24.04.5) with clang 18.1.3 and rustc 1.97.0. That machine has no `/sys/class/powercap` interface, so every energy field of the results file is recorded as unmeasured: the file holds no joules, no energy analysis is computed from it, and H4 is untested. All 16 programs gave the reference value on every declared input under every configuration, natively and as WebAssembly, and ε was counted for all 96 rows. Per call, ε ranges from 272,048 bits (`popcount` at `-O2` and `-O3` in C, and at `opt-level` 1, 2 and 3 in Rust) to 10,057,528 bits (Rust `matmul` at `opt-level=0`), a modelled Landauer bound at 300 K of 7.81×10⁻¹⁶ J to 2.89×10⁻¹⁴ J per call. Each of the 16 programs has its highest ε at level 0. Size optimisation does not always lower it: C `popcount` at `-Oz` counts 3,540,016 bits, about 13 times its count at `-O2`, and executes 488,404 WebAssembly instructions per call against 19,530. These counts say nothing yet about energy, and they are counted on WebAssembly, not on the native code an energy counter would read.

**E6 and E7** (H6 and H7) are planned, with no protocol yet. E6 needs a compiler built on the gear ladder, which does not exist. E5 has run, and §8.3 reports it.

### 8.3 E5: a name-free hash, measured

**Method.** E5 tests H5 and implements part R of the Compiler Form as two operations [6]. Its protocol and harness are dated 2 November 2026, before its first run, and the registration records E5's predictions and falsifiers under H5 on the same date. Each operation hashes a build output after removing what names the identifiers a program binds.

- *At the machine-code level*, a function identifier in the style of WARP, following the parts of WARP's published description that the E5 protocol quotes, is computed over unlinked object files: x86-64 ELF objects, arm64 Mach-O objects and WebAssembly objects. Each function is split into basic blocks; every instruction that a relocation record (a note telling the linker to patch an address there) points into is replaced by zero bytes; no-operation instructions and moves of a full-width register to itself are left out; each block is hashed, and the function's identifier is a hash of its blocks' hashes. Two keys come from it: the identifier of the kernel's function `run`, and a hash of the sorted identifiers of every function in the object, which no name enters.
- *At the IR level*, a hash of linked WebAssembly modules modulo alpha-equivalence (two programs are alpha-equivalent when a one-to-one renaming of bound identifiers turns one into the other) writes each module in a canonical form and hashes it with SHA-256. In that form a type index becomes the signature it names, locals are numbered by position and first use, functions are ordered by the shape of the call graph, independent definitions are sorted, and custom sections, such as the one that holds names, are left out. One key covers the whole module and one the part that `run` reaches. The protocol credits the idea of hashing modulo alpha-equivalence to Maziarz and colleagues (PLDI 2021) and states that its canonical form is not their algorithm.

Two baseline keys stand for the two kinds of cache key described under U in §3.5: the SHA-256 of the output bytes, and the SHA-256 of the source text and build command with the build directory removed. H5's metric names the compiler's own key; E5 reads no compiler's cache key and uses the two baselines in its place, a substitution the table of changes records. E5 compares keys; it runs no cache.

**Corpus and pairs.** The corpus is E4's 16 programs at their six levels, each with four outputs (an x86-64 object, an arm64 Mach-O object for C only, a WebAssembly object, and E4's linked WebAssembly module), and E1's C and Rust programs (`hello.c`, `sort.c`, `hello.rs` and `generic.rs`) at the same six levels, as x86-64 objects only. Each source is built from directory A, its same bytes from directory B, and a renamed copy from directory N; the three paths have equal length. The renamer gives every identifier the program binds a new name derived from the SHA-256 of the old one, and removes comments. The evidence that a renamed source is the same program is its behaviour: the linked modules of all three variants give E4's reference value on every declared input (96 of 96 in each variant), and each of E1's four programs prints the same bytes before and after renaming. A against B (*two directories*) and A against N (*renamed*) are declared equal. Every pair of levels of one toolchain, the C and Rust builds of one kernel at matching levels, and every pair of builds of two different kernels are declared not equal, as are the *mutations*: each linked module against itself with one opcode changed to another of its class, one constant changed or one memory offset changed.

A *hit* is an equal key on a two-directory or renamed pair. A *false hit* is an equal key on builds of two different kernels, or an IR hash left unchanged by a mutation. An equal key on two levels, or on the C and Rust builds of one kernel, is reported and not counted as false, because each such pair computes one function. H5 is supported if, on the renamed pairs, every R hash has a higher hit rate than both baselines and there is no false hit; it is falsified by any false hit, or if no R hash has a higher hit rate than both baselines on the renamed pairs.

**Machine.** E5 ran on 2 November 2026 (UTC) on a Linux x86-64 machine (Intel Xeon at 2.80 GHz, 4 CPUs, Ubuntu 24.04.5) with clang 18.1.3 and rustc 1.97.0. It built and hashed 360 x86-64 objects, 144 Mach-O objects, 288 WebAssembly objects and 288 linked modules. The instruction boundaries found by its x86-64 decoder agreed with those of LLVM's `llvm-objdump` in all 386 functions of the x86-64 objects built in directory A.

**Results.** Equal keys, out of the pairs compared. The R hash is the machine-code identifier for objects and the IR hash for linked modules.

**Findings.**

1. **No false hit.** No R hash was equal on any of 26,208 comparisons of builds of two different kernels (4,032 pairs per output and key, 1,008 for the Mach-O objects), and none of 9,328 mutations of 96 linked modules (4,712 of an opcode, 3,564 of a constant and 1,052 of a memory offset) left the IR hash unchanged.
2. **A gain on renamed pairs.** On the renamed pairs every R hash, in each of its nine combinations of output and key, had a higher hit rate than both baselines: 100% for the machine-code identifiers, against 45.8% to 70.8% for output bytes and 0% to 50.0% for the source key; 90.6% for the IR hashes, against 79.2% and 0%.
3. **The verdict.** By its registered conditions, H5 is supported on this corpus. The table of changes records the verdict together with the predictions that did not hold, and limits it to this corpus, two toolchains and one IR [6].
4. **Misses at the IR level.** The IR hashes were unequal on 9 of the 96 two-directory pairs and 9 of the 96 renamed pairs: the linked Rust modules of all eight kernels at `opt-level=0`, and Rust `sort` at `opt-level=1`. In each, only the data part of the canonical form differs, and each module holds one copy of its build directory's path. The registered IR hash treats data bytes as constants and does not remove the build path, which the Form's R removes. A diagnostic key added after the first run, which writes that path in the data as a placeholder and is not used for the verdict, is equal on all 96 two-directory pairs and on 92 of the 96 renamed pairs. The four left are Rust `fib`, `matmul`, `sieve` and `sort` at `opt-level=0`, again in their data. For `fib`, a check outside the harness found the two data segments differing in the column numbers of the location records that Rust's overflow messages carry: the renamed identifiers are longer, so the same expression starts at a later column.
5. **Other predictions that did not hold.** The source key was equal on 12 of the 24 renamed pairs of E1's programs, because the two hello programs bind no identifier and the renamer left them unchanged. The machine-code identifier of `run` in the WebAssembly objects was equal for the C and the Rust build of `fib` at five matching levels (1, 2, 3, s and z) and of `popcount` at s, 6 of 48 pairs; each such pair computes one function, so it is not a false hit. On the linked modules the IR hashes differ in all 48 C and Rust pairs; the functions part of their canonical forms is equal in 6 of the 48, and the memory part in none.
6. **Levels.** Across pairs of optimisation levels of one toolchain, the R hashes were equal on 10.0% to 21.7% of pairs and the output bytes on 10.0% to 12.5%, as registered: unequal on most pairs, with equal pairs reported and not counted.

**What E5 does not show.** An equal machine-code identifier establishes no equivalence on its own: a zeroed instruction can call or load a different symbol once the object is linked, and the identifier does not see which. WARP's constraints, which tell such functions apart, are not implemented, so a machine-code hit is a candidate for reuse that a checker still has to accept, and the protocol gives these identifiers no property class. An equal IR hash means that the two linked modules are alpha-equivalent, barring a SHA-256 collision, which the protocol takes as the exact level for their integer results under the WebAssembly semantics; it says nothing about the native code built from the same source. Unequal hashes say nothing about inequivalence: pairs of levels compute one function under different hashes, and two alpha-equivalent modules whose functions tie in the canonical order could be ordered differently, since ties are counted, not resolved. If an IR hash keyed reuse at rung G0, the protocol's gate statement would be equivalence at the exact level of the module, linked by its import and export names by a host that does not read custom sections, with E5's own readers, canonicaliser and hashing code, Python's hashing libraries and the WebAssembly specification as the trusted base. That no false hit occurred among these pairs and mutations does not show that the hashes have none on other programs: the corpus is 16 kernels and 4 programs, from two toolchains that both lower through LLVM, run on one machine, and the IR is WebAssembly, not LLVM IR. And since E5 reads no compiler's own cache key, it does not show that any compiler's cache would gain hits.

## 9. Limits of this study

- **Almost all external evidence is [S] or [K].** The hosts that hold the primary texts could not be reached from the research sessions, the GitHub commits API with a path filter was refused, and the national databases (ACM DL, CNKI, J-STAGE, eLibrary.ru, HAL) were reached only through general web search. Before publication beyond a draft, the primary texts need reading, in this order: Hoare, He and Sampaio (1993); Ershov (1977); the theorem statement in the CompCert manual; arXiv:2610.33193, to settle PP-complete against PP-hard; arXiv:2609.11356; and the Zig release notes.
- **The negative search for a named law is open in some languages.** Native terms for "law of compilation" in Chinese, Russian, French and German were not searched one by one.
- **The per-part table is qualitative.** It summarises architecture and documentation; it is not a measurement of each compiler.
- **No energy has been measured.** E4 has counted erasure and description length on a machine that exposes no energy counter (§8.2), so its results hold no joules and H4 is untested; its energy runs need a host with RAPL or `powermetrics`.
- **The measured corpora are small.** E1 builds one or two programs per language; E5 hashes 16 kernels and 4 programs from two toolchains that both lower through LLVM, on one machine, with WebAssembly as its one IR (§8.3).
- **The 98/2 split is registered, not yet measured.** It is hypothesis H6 (§5, §7), and its measurement is planned rung by rung with a declared unit.

## 10. Conclusion

Measured against real compilers, the candidate splits into two kinds of part. Representation, cost, checker, reuse and gate describe practice that exists, unevenly, somewhere in the field. Lowering, the convergence function and energy describe a standard no shipping compiler meets. That split is the reason to restate the candidate as a normal form: placing gcc, rustc, Go, GHC, XLA and the rest against one declared contract, with path-stripped determinism, named gates, explicit equivalence levels and a measured distance from two computable bounds, would give compilers from these communities a common scale. R, the part no compiler surveyed has, is the first the project has built and measured: on E4's small corpus, a name-free hash at the machine-code level and one at the IR level matched renamed programs more often than keys over output bytes or source text, and matched no builds of two different kernels (§8.3). That supports H5 for this corpus only, and an equal machine-code identifier still establishes no equivalence without a checker behind it. The weakest link is the physics. E4 has counted erasure and description length for 96 builds, but has read no energy. With hardware $10^6$ to $10^{11}$ above the floor, the erasure and description-length experiment may show that neither count predicts energy outside regime C. That result would still settle something: RCE Compile would rank by measured joules and keep Landauer only as a bound. The 98/2 principle needs the same treatment. The literature supports its two premises, gate everything and put deterministic reuse first, but says nothing about the number, so the unit it is counted in has to be chosen and published before the first measurement.
